### 🔍 1.1 Site Description
The tower stands on the hilltop at the centre of Ayelet Hashachar's horseshoe. This is the core of the kibbutz as ICA planned it, in the southern Hula Valley, and it lies inside the declared antiquities areas on the edge of Tel Hazor [Doc:6, 8].

ICA's technical bureau designed the tower [User; Doc:44]. In 1923 the "Ahva" company of the Labour Battalion built it under technician Michael Ehrlich, together with the first cowshed and five stone houses [Doc:17–18].

The tower is a rectangular reinforced-concrete tank of about 25 m³ on four columns, with a tie beam halfway up and thickened bases. Framed panels, low gables and corner blocks decorate the tank [Doc:17; Img]. A concrete plaque on the north face, which faces the horseshoe houses, names the Public Works Office, Ahva and Ehrlich [Doc:17, 38]. A pump lifted spring water up from Wadi Waqqas, and the tower then fed taps outside the houses by gravity [Doc:9, 17].

Later changes [Doc:13, 21, 24]:
- 1936: a dining hall was built just north of it.
- 1936–39: a signalling post replaced the gables, and the legs were apparently walled in at about the same time.
- From 1937: the tower took well water through the "500" pool, and later from an underground pool.
- After 1945: a bakery was built against it, and the dining-hall complex closed around it.

It went out of use in the 1960s [Doc:21; User]. In 2012 its render was peeling and its plaque was cracking around rusting anchors [Doc:37–38]. It has since been listed and restored, and the bakery oven beside it remains and is occasionally used [User].

### 🌐 1.3 Contexts

⚔️ **Political: ICA and the kvutza competing over what the settlement should become.**
- ICA designed the tower [User] within a horseshoe plan meant to convert into a moshav if the commune broke up [Art:322–324]. Read this way, the tower is ICA infrastructure at the centre of a plan built for dissolution 〰️.
- The plaque names the labour builders and not ICA [Doc:17]. Set against ICA's drawing, the tower may itself record that contest 💭 [Doc:44].
- 🧭 Planning: interpretation should present both authorships, ICA's design and labour's construction.

🌿 **Environmental: water as the settlement's limiting resource.**
- Water held the settlement back for two decades: tins carried by donkey, a pipe in 1921, disputed spring rights, wells from 1936, and Mekorot from 1951 [Doc:7–9].
- This frames the tower as the first pressurised point in a water system later extended by the channel, the pools and the wells 〰️ [Doc:8, 21].
- 🧭 Planning: interpret the tower together with the parts of that system still visible in 2012: the spring pool, the channel, the "500" pool and Well A [Doc:10].

👥 **Social: the Labour Battalion and "conquest of labour".**
- The Ahva group came from Kovno and learned to build on the job. Peres's diary records sinking columns and cracking walls in the cowshed and houses [Art:328; Doc:18–19].
- The plaque, and the 1923 relief on the cowshed, write the builders' group into the fabric itself [Doc:18]. That frames the tower as a record of their work, not only of its function 〰️.

🏔 **Landscape: hilltop landmark, then signalling post, then hidden.**
- Placed at the hilltop centre as a prominent landmark [Doc:8], the tower looked out over the Hula Valley towards Mt Hermon [Img].
- The same height served the signalling post in 1936–39 [Doc:21].
- The dining hall and the bakery later hid it from view [Doc:21, 24]. Its position thus accounts both for its early prominence and for its later disappearance into the complex 〰️.

⚙️ **Technological: early reinforced concrete, with a decorated tank.**
- Reinforced-concrete water towers first appeared in the country in 1912–14, and a wave followed in the 1920s [Doc:25].
- Shusterman notes this tower's unusual decorative treatment compared with the plain functional norm [Doc:17].
- With ICA as designer [User], the decoration reads as ICA's choice rather than Ehrlich's 〰️. It may echo the colony-style houses ICA built at the same time 💭 [Art:324].
