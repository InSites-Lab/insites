# Cultural-InSites — a course study with non-heritage students

**Cultural-InSites: A Human-AI Collaboration Framework for Teaching Significance Assessment for Digital Heritage Storytelling**
Chao Zhou, Eugene Ch'ng, Yael Alef, Yuval Shafriri. Manuscript under review.

The manuscript: [`CulturalInSites_manuscript.pdf`](CulturalInSites_manuscript.pdf)

## The study in brief

Students of animation and interactive media at Beijing Normal-Hong Kong Baptist University (BNBU) used *InSites* during a one-semester digital heritage course. They had little formal training in heritage studies. They assessed the cultural significance of heritage places and objects through the staged workflow (Stages 0–6, with a human checkpoint after each stage), then translated the assessment into digital storytelling projects. The study asks how far the workflow supported their ability and confidence in significance assessment (RQ1), and what role human judgement played within the AI-supported reasoning (RQ2).

The evidence comes from three sources: a post-use survey (N = 25), the students' technical reports and project artefacts, and the staged assessment conversations. It is a single-cohort, course-embedded evaluation without a comparison condition, and the survey measures self-reported perception.

## What the paper reports

- **Confidence.** Self-rated confidence in identifying heritage values rose from M = 4.20 to M = 5.88 on a seven-point scale (paired t(24) = 5.85, p < .001). The before-use rating was retrospective.
- **Perceived improvement across assessment domains.** Students most often reported improvement in heritage value identification (19/25, 76%) and least often in connecting evidence to argument (8/25, 32%). Comparative evaluation (68%), contextual analysis (64%), significance statement writing (64%) and authenticity and integrity (56%) fell in between.
- **Reading the gap.** The assessment outputs themselves typically anchored claims to named source files, so the weak evidence-argument result is read as a gap in what students carried into their own reasoning rather than as a limitation of the outputs.
- **Engagement at the checkpoints varied.** Some students steered the reasoning by supplying cited evidence that prompted a stage revision, correcting outputs or redirecting the assessment toward documented history. Others advanced with little intervention, and in one case evidence gathering was delegated to the model. The paper concludes that human judgement cannot be assumed simply because checkpoints are present; it depends on the criteria learners are given for evaluating evidence, values and interpretive claims.
- **From significance to storytelling.** Support was perceived as stronger for structuring evidence and identifying values than for translating significance into narrative and design decisions.

The findings are in the sections *Perceived Improvement across CBSA*, *Human Judgement in AI-Supported CBSA Reasoning* and *Artefact-Based Evidence from Student Reports and AI Outputs*, and are discussed under *Human Judgement and the Boundary of AI-Supported Assessment*.
